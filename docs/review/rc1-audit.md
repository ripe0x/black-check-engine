# Checks Survival Protocol rc1 — adversarial security audit

**Target and isolation.** This is a fresh discovery run on the exported `rc1` tree at commit `0aeea0e686f358839dfbc79b5a8aec5896897f4d`, not a review of `main`. The original checkout was not edited. The export is a local filesystem copy, **not an independently isolated agent or evidence boundary**. Excluded paths were deleted before source analysis; the only permitted CLAUDE section was later extracted through a filtering command into [permitted-invariants.md](/private/tmp/rc1/audit/permitted-invariants.md). No earlier audit, incident, test (other than the two allowed harnesses), or earlier-session agent output was read. During submodule checkout Git printed four commit *subjects*, an expressly excluded input, so under the owner's rule this run is **informed**. The subject text was not used as evidence. A delegated reviewer worked in a separate export with clean task context and no access to our findings before its discovery result; the shared host filesystem did **not** enforce blindness. The reviewer independently found the venue launch lockout and Shape-recipient custody loss. No transactions were broadcast.

**Pinned chain.** Ethereum mainnet block `26,305,656`, hash `0x3ce084147911086f57fa819f0b765bd2fc31e23b1bca33d357a252dff2e12228`, timestamp 2026-10-30 12:45:23 UTC. Every Ethereum RPC call and fork used `https://mainnet.gateway.tenderly.co`.

**Outcome.** Three high-impact failure classes were reproduced on the pinned fork: public extraction of buyback value through a burner sandwich, permanent custody loss by naming an inert contract as the FWA purchaser, and a non-council deployer's ability to block or unilaterally trigger coin-market launch. A conditional pre-funding theft in the FWA fee helper was also reproduced. This is a **bounded, incomplete examination**: the positive fork checks below constrain other paths but do not establish safety or release readiness. LaunchConfig contains unset production roles and a placeholder coin name and economic values, so the fork substituted test roles and did not model a finalized launch.

## System and rights

The engine buys Checks Originals and Editions with a capped acquisition budget, turns Editions into Originals, lists Checks and ETH-backed Shapes through an engine-only controller in an FWA V2 pool, counts real prize allocations survived, composes eligible Checks, and permits exact-price ETH or coin-burn buyouts. The engine holds ETH as acquisition budget, game reserve, and surplus; FWA holds listed NFTs, acquisition escrow, owner proceeds, and player delivery/refund claims; Shapes holds ETH backing; the controller can hold FWA fee tokens and FWA proceeds; the venue locks the coin supply in Uniswap v4; the hook holds ETH swap fees until forwarding them to the acquisition budget; and the burner holds released surplus ETH until it swaps and burns acquired coin. A separate helper receives ETH to buy the 50,000 FWA creation fee into the controller.

Users may contribute and sell NFTs, buy draws, receive or refund a won item through FWA, trigger maintenance, buy a listed Check at the published price, and burn their own coin for eligible actions. They may not gain another user's entitlement, move locked liquidity, change prices or authority, or withdraw treasury assets outside a valid sale. The council is the sole timelock proposer; the guardian can cancel, halt, and resume; the operator may halt only. Sensitive engine changes and UUPS upgrades require a council proposal plus seven days without guardian cancellation. The controller permanently owns its FWA pool and may act on it only for the engine. The venue deployer wires and launches once; the hook's initialization gate binds that path. The engine approves canonical Checks to consume Editions it owns, and coin-paid actions depend on the player's allowance.

The forbidden states tested or examined were unauthorized treasury/NFT extraction, authority without the timelock, counterfeit survival, coin inflation or double burning, fees leaving the acquisition budget, removable liquidity, unwinnable or stranded listed assets, impaired player delivery/refund rights, stale-rule prices, and custody/accounting divergence. Council-majority action after the full delay and trusted FWA, canonical Checks/Shapes, and Uniswap behavior were treated as accepted trust, not findings by themselves.

## Confirmed findings

### H1 — Permissionless burner execution can be sandwiched for ETH profit

`Burner.burn()` spends up to `maxPerBurn` ETH on the venue with a minimum output of **zero**. Anyone can choose the transaction's timing. An attacker buys coin, calls `burn()`, then sells in one transaction. The burner buys after the attacker's price move and receives fewer coins; the attacker realizes ETH profit from the public surplus. The constructor's chunk cap and `interval` bound one invocation but do not prevent repeated extraction over funded intervals. This is contrary to the permitted functional specification's restricted buyback route requiring slippage and independent price checks, and economically reduces permanent burns. The source comment acknowledges the sandwich exposure; that acknowledgment does not protect assets.

The executable fork reproduction is `testPermissionlessBurnCanBeSandwichedForProfit` in [AuditFork.t.sol](/private/tmp/rc1/test/audit/AuditFork.t.sol). It deploys the rc1 venue, approves the burner through a real council schedule and seven-day delay, releases 10 ETH of engine surplus, then runs a single-transaction buy/burn/sell. With a 10 ETH attacker front buy, attacker profit was **1.554294868211295010 ETH**. A baseline burner invocation destroyed `90,171,966.116133743196946695` coin; the attacked invocation destroyed `75,255,083.778355860531882349`, about **16.54% fewer**. The hook received `0.316710049173851464 ETH` in fees and forwarded it to the engine acquisition budget; these fees did not erase the attacker profit. The 10 ETH surplus and 100 ETH attacker starting balance are test-funded. This is one market state, not a maximum-loss estimate.

Affected rc1 source is [Burner.sol](/private/tmp/rc1/src/treasury/Burner.sol:61) at commit above. Its creation bytecode Keccak-256 is `0xeb07e36c1b60049ab751b982ce8dccc3941cea7fe8fd4a2303a7aca0e2da580c`; its constructor-filled runtime in the tested deployment hashes to `0xb3078a6a4070b82ae1aca9042183ba31b0eb49f21edd0e0712181cd4148e2b3d`.

### H2 — An arbitrary FWA purchaser can strand a won Check or backed Shape

The deployed FWA V2 pool accepts a `purchaser` address supplied by the caller, distinct from the payer. The protocol lists irreplaceable Checks and ETH-backed Shapes into that pool without a recipient-safety boundary. In `testAdversarialPurchaserCanStrandListedCheck`, a buyer named the canonical Shapes contract as purchaser for a pool containing Check #12. A real FWA allocation delivered the Check to Shapes. The Check ceased to be listed, Shapes became `ownerOf(12)`, and `Engine.recoverCheck` reverted because FWA had settled the delivery. The verified Shapes runtime exposes no route to transfer an unrelated Check, so this Check is stranded absent an external privileged intervention or future code change. In a second variant, `testPoolSelfRecipientSettlesCheckWithoutReleasingCustody`, the buyer named the FWA pool itself. FWA marked the Check **Settled** while `ownerOf` still returned the pool; engine recovery and the pool-owner withdrawal path both reverted. This variant does not depend on Shapes' behavior.

In `testShapeDeliveryFailureForcesRestockAndStrandsBacking`, a buyer named Shapes itself as purchaser for a Shape. Canonical Shapes rejected self-transfer; FWA marked delivery pending and kept the backed Shape. After the delivery deadline the payer could not refund, because FWA reserves that call for the named purchaser; the purchaser is Shapes and has no method to make the call. Engine `recoverCheck` does not recover Shapes. Permissionless `restockShapes` then minted a replacement and spent **0.011 ETH** of test-funded game capital while the original `0.01 ETH` backed Shape remained in FWA custody. The buyer repeated the same draw against that replacement; a second Shape became pending, and a second restock spent another **0.011 ETH**. After two cycles, **0.022 ETH** of test-funded game capital had funded two restocks, two backed Shapes were stuck in FWA custody, and FWA owner proceeds remained zero because both purchase prices were escrowed. This demonstrates two cycles, not an unbounded drain rate. A snapshot branch retired the controller through a real seven-day council operation and confirmed the first pending delivery and failed recovery persisted; it then reverted to the snapshot for the replacement sequence. Indefinite stranding additionally depends on the verified Shapes call surface and unchanged contracts. The attacker pays the FWA acquisition price and may lose that payment, so this is griefing and inventory/capital harm, not a demonstrated attacker profit.

Both reproductions are in [AuditFork.t.sol](/private/tmp/rc1/test/audit/AuditFork.t.sol). The integration path is [Engine.sol](/private/tmp/rc1/src/core/Engine.sol:183) through [FwaV2Controller.sol](/private/tmp/rc1/src/fwa/FwaV2Controller.sol:89) into the FWA pool's `acquire`/delivery logic. The affected engine implementation's tested runtime hash is `0x5473f0e903ed4868523481fe372043cb433791065e8f26d6a0d9d281fc7b9848`, controller runtime `0xe3c37dea766b0128dd541ca807c4975dfd74f29b02684ea33c2df9e50df226b3`, and FWA implementation at the pinned block `0x84dee03dd3af8ee9fb96df379991b881988526db09cb7e294c136fedf9a011c5` (address `0xEaa4d032908A9734a2c7939c0D211c35ad51eb31`). The FWA behavior itself is trusted; the finding is the protocol's exposure of its listed assets to a caller-selected inert recipient.

### H3 — A non-council deployer controls launch and can permanently block it

`DeploySystem` constructs `VenueLiquidity` from the deploying address, and `VenueLiquidity` permanently records that address as its sole `launch()` caller. `postflight` requires the venue to remain **unlaunched** and rejects the deployer as a timelock proposer or canceller. The deployer can therefore open the coin market unilaterally at any chosen time, while a lost or withholding deployer can leave the entire initially minted coin supply held by an unlaunched venue with no exit and no council recovery function. The venue's immutable `startTick` prevents a price change, but it does not remove timing or liveness authority.

The independent reviewer reproduced this in a separate export at block 26,305,869, hash `0xa79dcb369d2a6c671f3a61d74e1cf5f8205f8500f3558b5040bfcf1a5c50bbb5`. I then reproduced it at this report's pinned block in `testCouncilCannotLaunchIfDeployerWithholds`: a council proposal waited seven full days, but timelock execution reverted with `VenueLiquidity.NotDeployer`; guardian launch also reverted; `launched` stayed false and the venue retained all coin. Other fork tests show the deployer can call `launch` without a council proposal. This is a pre-launch failure mode. If the deployer actually launches in the intended window, the denial branch is no longer reachable.

Affected rc1 code is [VenueLiquidity.sol](/private/tmp/rc1/src/venue/VenueLiquidity.sol:93) with [DeploySystem.sol](/private/tmp/rc1/script/DeploySystem.sol:70). VenueLiquidity creation hash is `0x76f861ed75e5eddc0eb21dc2dfe9b7cb90555b55babd50451d0cec15bff9b57b`; its constructor-filled runtime in the tested production wiring is `0xd880ffea092086faa20ce313fb17f8be404616be9c38cb849c7e96af249d623d`.

### M1 — Any caller can take ETH pre-funded to the FWA fee buyer

`FwaFeeBuyer.buyTo()` refunds `address(this).balance`, not the residual ETH from its caller's own swap. If ETH was sent directly or left in the helper, the next caller can buy a trivial amount of FWA with `minOut=0` and receive the entire pre-existing balance. In `testPrefundedFeeBuyerCanBeDrainedByCaller`, the helper was pre-funded with 1 ETH, and an attacker calling with 0.01 ETH ended 0.99 ETH ahead of its pre-call balance while the helper ended at zero. This requires pre-funded ETH; the production deploy routine does not itself fund the helper. A forced or mistaken transfer creates the condition. See [FwaFeeBuyer.sol](/private/tmp/rc1/src/periphery/FwaFeeBuyer.sol:34), rc1 creation hash `0x643be64eacee1d8280acb48fc7a11a900147b7747c71b6a25b0c37a01702e0a6`, tested constructor-filled runtime hash `0xf4b2db205dd9dceae9cb865f6d6d986eabe75ed4f067340ecea95e36e4ce42b5`.

**Deployment integration risk, without a demonstrated adversary gain:** `FwaFeeBuyer` swaps exact ETH input. In a test-funded 100 ETH buy, it delivered `24,780,353.144182860972764547` FWA to the controller; opening the pool consumed exactly 50,000 FWA and left `24,730,353.144182860972764547` FWA there. The controller has no FWA withdrawal or sale method. This is a severe oversizing example, not a prediction of the final funding amount or a separate attacker-controlled extraction. The final ETH input and slippage floor are unset, so the actual stranded amount remains unresolved.

**Deployment gate defect:** The permitted founder invariant says the public coin name and ticker are unresolved and the system must not launch under the existing Black Check project's name. `LaunchConfig.mainnet()` still supplies `"Placeholder Coin"` and `"BLACK"`; [DeploySystem.sol](/private/tmp/rc1/script/DeploySystem.sol:112) checks roles, proxy, custody, factory and venue state but never checks the coin identity. `testProductionWiringCodeHashes` explicitly asserted both placeholders while `postflight` passed. The no-broadcast canonical-CREATE2 script rehearsal also passed with them. This is a release-gate failure, not a demonstrated asset theft, and final identity input remains to be chosen. The affected deployment source is rc1; `DeploySystem` is abstract with no runtime output, and `LaunchConfig` creation output hashes to `0x512f480ab92182c6d073da377db24c4beb6454889b98a24f24e9daaf23a78066`.

## Method, tests, and bounded exclusions

Slither 0.11.5 enumerated entry points, writes, external calls, and call graphs from this rc1 export. Its JSON run exited 255 because it reported 269 detections, not because compilation failed; the printer run exited 0. I inspected the 73 High/Medium leads by source and class and retained one bounded assessment per detector in [slither-triage.json](/private/tmp/rc1/audit/slither-triage.json). `_payBid`'s arbitrary recipient is the actual NFT seller after an ownership transfer and a fixed standing-bid/cap check; a hostile seller callback failed to reenter. `restockShapes`' ETH call is within `nonReentrant` and goes to the pinned Shapes code. Reentrancy warnings around list/withdraw/reprice/setController depend on trusted canonical/FWA calls or the full-delay controller approval; they do not by themselves establish an outsider callback path. The zero-balance strict equalities, uninitialized local `fee` (Solidity initializes it to zero), and project-level unused returns were inspected. Slither findings are leads, not negative proof, and its library-level detections were not treated as findings in trusted Uniswap code. [slither.json](/private/tmp/rc1/audit/slither.json) and [slither-printers.err](/private/tmp/rc1/audit/slither-printers.err) preserve output. The audit-standard source inventory yielded 60 Engine entrypoints and 61 external calls, plus inventories for the six other concrete in-scope contracts; the abstract DeploySystem inventory failed because the utility reported two matches for its single source name. No schema-2 gate PASS is claimed: a complete manually evidenced gate manifest and independent evidence challenge were not produced. The audit method is **uncalibrated**; no hidden-case evaluation or competent human review occurred.

The separate reviewer used its own export and pinned block. It independently fork-tested the deployer lockout and Shape self-recipient loss, and a normal Shape delivery control. Its first turn was interrupted by a tool safety error; its subsequent bounded final left survival permutations, burns, bids, fee routing, and source reconstruction open. Our tests and primary-chain/source checks addressed those paths only to the explicit bounds in this report. The reviewer was not a human sign-off and its shared-host access did not enforce process blindness.

The strongest counterexamples and exclusions were exercised by [AuditFork.t.sol](/private/tmp/rc1/test/audit/AuditFork.t.sol) with `--match-path`; the final integrated focused run passed **24/24** tests, including **16/16** burner-front-size and **16/16** venue-fee fuzz cases. All tests deploy with `DeploySystem.deploySystem` and call its `postflight`.
